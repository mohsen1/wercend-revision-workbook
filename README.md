# Reverse outline worksheet: find the paragraph your reader needed first

A free revision exercise from [Wercend](https://wercend.com/?utm_source=github&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=revision_workbook). Bring a draft, a pen, and one reader in mind. You can do the whole exercise on paper.

[Print the one-page worksheet for a writing group](https://github.com/mohsen1/wercend-revision-workbook/releases/download/v1.0/reverse-outline-worksheet.pdf) or [copy the editable worksheet](worksheet.md).

A reverse outline is an outline you make *after* writing. The [University of Wisconsin–Madison Writing Center](https://writing.wisc.edu/handbook/processandstructure/reverseoutlines/) explains how to use paragraph summaries to examine the order of a draft. This workbook adds a reader’s question beside each summary. Sometimes a paragraph is in the right place for the writer who knows the ending and the wrong place for someone arriving cold.

## Try it on three paragraphs

Imagine finding this notice taped to the door of your local library. You're carrying a book to return.

> **A.** On Monday, the library will begin a six-week trial of its new returns system. The board will review the results in June.
>
> **B.** Volunteers spent Saturday hanging signs and painting arrows. Once the trial ends, staff will ask visitors which signs they noticed.
>
> **C.** If you usually return books at the front desk, take them to the side door on Maple Street instead. The front entrance is closed for repairs.

Cover paragraph C. After A and B, could you return your book? Now imagine that the reader is a board member opening a meeting about the trial. Would you still start in the same place?

| Passage | What the reader learns | What they still need |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | There is a trial, with a review in June. | Does this change what I do today? |
| B | Someone put up signs. | Which way do I go? |
| C | Returns move to Maple Street during repairs. | When does the change begin? |

**One revision for the door:** “Starting Monday, return books at the side door on Maple Street. The front entrance will be closed for repairs.” Move the board’s review date into the meeting notes. Leave out the volunteers unless the notice also has a reason to thank them.

**One revision for the meeting:** “The board will review the six-week returns trial in June. After the trial, staff will ask visitors which signs they noticed.” The route belongs on the notice; what the board will review belongs on the agenda. Both revisions use the same facts.

## Use it on your own draft

1. Write down a particular reader and what they came to find out. “A tenant holding the rent increase letter” gives you more to work with than “the general public.”
2. Number the paragraphs. Beside each, write one short sentence describing what changes for that reader. If two ideas in a paragraph do different jobs, give them separate rows.
3. Read only those sentences, in order. Mark the first place you need a fact, reason, or definition that hasn't appeared yet. Suspense can be deliberate; mark it only if the gap keeps you from following the next idea.
4. Move the smallest passage that closes the gap. Read the old and new openings aloud. What can the reader understand or do sooner? What, if anything, did the move spoil?

## Run it with a writing partner in ten minutes

Bring a draft of three to five paragraphs. Keep a copy of the original so you can compare the two openings at the end.

| Time | Writer | Reader |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–2 minutes | Write down what you hope the reader will understand by the end. Fold that note over. | Read the draft once. Pause after each paragraph to write the question you have at that moment. |
| 2–5 minutes | Listen and mark the first place your reader needed more context. | Read your questions aloud in order. Point to the words that raised each one. |
| 5–8 minutes | Move or revise one passage. | Try the new order. Say where a question gets answered earlier or where a surprise loses its force. |
| 8–10 minutes | Unfold your note and compare it with what your reader took away. | Identify one sentence you would keep in either version, and say why. |

If you want to test a reveal on someone coming to the story fresh, give the revised version to a second reader. Your first reader already knows the ending.
